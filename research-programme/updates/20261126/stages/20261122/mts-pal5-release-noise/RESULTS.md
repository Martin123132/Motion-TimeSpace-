## What is new: a shared release clock in measurement units

Use just the two mock cluster-relative line-of-sight velocities, one recently released tracer in each tail. Their frozen means on three release dates, two signs, two preparations, three distance assumptions and two previously saved numerical grids are unchanged. For an intentionally optimistic test assign each mock tracer independently the *smallest* of the 109 quoted spectroscopic errors from [Kuzma et al. extended Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04548): 0.22 km/s; also repeat at the catalogue median 1.39 km/s. Assume an exactly known cluster, no intrinsic velocity scatter, known distance and preparation and equal sign odds. These are error scales, not an association of either mock tracer with a specific catalogue star. No observed radial velocity was used in this check. This is a distinct comparison from the gradient along the full mock stream.

At the nominal distance and central release date, divide the two-velocity separation by the adopted 0.22 error. This gives 0.000268 (reset) and 0.007260 (inherited). Give the rival sign a single release-date shift shared by both tails, restricted to ±10 Myr, using piecewise-linear interpolation of its saved velocities. For forward injected, the least-squares distances become 0.0000767 and 0.0000804, at shifts +0.00234 and +0.06640 Myr. Reversing the injection on the same grid gives 0.0000767 and 0.0000803 at −0.00217 and −0.06225 Myr. These shifts are interpolation diagnostics, not stellar ages inferred to that precision. Fitting the *six coordinates* equally relative to their own same-date differences is a different question: earlier checks (not bundled here) show one common date cannot generally mimic the entire orbit.

**Upper bound without inventing a stripping-time prior.** With a revealed date, two Gaussian means with shared covariance have ideal equal-odds accuracy Φ(d/2), where d is their noise-weighted distance. Within each saved linear segment d is the norm of an affine function, hence its maximum occurs at an endpoint. For *any* common distribution of release time over those segments, hiding the date cannot outperform revealing it: the oracle's average accuracy is no larger than its maximum. Across all three distances and all the saved release dates, even revealing the date gives at most **50.094% reset / 50.274% inherited** on the finer grid using the best quoted error. This ceiling is for those two velocities, not for all stream stars, arbitrary stripping physics, sign-dependent escape histories or the 17-star astrometric comparison. There is no justification for combining the tests as independent evidence or extrapolating 111 Myr linearly over gigayears.
