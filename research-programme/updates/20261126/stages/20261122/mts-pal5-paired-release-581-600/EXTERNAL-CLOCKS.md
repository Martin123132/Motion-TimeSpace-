# Independent references for the clock and launch comparison

There is no unique observed “lifetime of Pal 5”. The [Odenkirchen et al. tip drift estimates](https://arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0307446) concern the leading and trailing debris accessible in their footprints. [Fritz & Kallivayalil §IV.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1508.06647) assume 5 Gyr; they do not measure that duration. The joined 222.84 Myr is 17.69% of the 1.26 Gyr leading-tip estimate, 11.49% of the 1.94 Gyr trailing-tip estimate, and 4.46% of the 5 Gyr model. These are different denominators. Even within the paired field interval, the simulated pericentre releases have only about 126 Myr of flight; the nominal midpoint releases have 157–177 Myr.

**An additional, more recent empirical comparison.** [Chen, Li & Gnedin, Table 1, v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.19899v2) give a model-inferred duration of 6.2 ± 1.7 Gyr for their selected Pal 5 stream and a stellar loss rate of 2.7 ± 0.6 solar masses/Myr. The paired interval covers 3.59% of their nominal duration. They use a 23.6 kpc distance and a 17,000-solar-mass cluster. The inferred age and rate depend on a spray model, selection/completeness and stellar population; they are not individual release times or a mass history measured for our recent pericentre. Their input stream data overlap Pal 5 work. This is context, not a held-out score.

At r=60 pc, v=1 km/s, the point-mass threshold for a zero-binding launch is 6,975 solar masses. Relative to the conditional 4,297-solar-mass profile at the present epoch, a mass at least this large roughly 126 Myr earlier would require a mean total loss of at least 21 solar masses/Myr over that interval (larger for an extended spherical profile). This is a requirement, not an inference that the cluster did or did not lose that amount. Conversely the higher-mass catalogue convention would already exceed the threshold now. Do **not** combine the higher-mass model’s rate with the lower-mass profile to claim a discrepancy in σ, assume the same mass function or aperture, or assume steady dynamical loss equals total mass change. This is why profiling the progenitor independently is more informative than fitting another sign to the same two mock velocities.

For a concrete follow-up, [Chen et al.'s ejection calculation](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01496v2) distinguishes cluster-inertial velocity from velocity in a co-rotating frame and tests a correlated distribution of launch positions/directions against numerical clusters. It is a useful external control, not a new MTS coupling. [Gieles et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-021-01392-2) give a [public 11.5-Gyr snapshot](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4739181) of their best-matching simulation, not a public time series of every escape in the recent interval. The files were not needed for the present calculation. A single image or snapshot cannot decide how the initial phase space was populated.
